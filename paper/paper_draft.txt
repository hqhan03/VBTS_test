ABSTRACT
================================================================================

[ABS]
Vision-based tactile sensors encode contact as an image, buying dense spatial
information at the cost of a large data volume per sensor. That cost becomes a
system constraint as sensor count grows, raising aggregate bandwidth and
spending the latency budget reactive control needs; high-bandwidth
instrumentation has been named a fundamental obstacle for compact tactile
fingers [1]. Pixel density, in pixels per square millimetre of sensing surface,
is therefore fixed early, since it constrains camera, electronics and network
input size together. Designers currently have no basis for choosing it, because
the elastomer and the camera have never been varied in the same experiment. We
separate the two decisions using 27 sensors, one per condition, spanning two
sensing principles and a marker-bearing photometric variant, three hardness
levels and three sensing-layer thicknesses. Imaging hardware is reused within
each arm, and one 1920x1080 capture is downsampled across twelve densities. The
elastomer decides three things and we measure all three: paired-contact
separation, which degrades with thickness on every measure we record (Spearman
rho = -0.74 for maximum dip, p = 0.023); the force at which the image response
saturates, which moves with thickness in opposite directions in the two arms;
and how the imprint grows and brightens with indentation. Pixel density is
decided by the task instead. Across twenty tests of a thickness or hardness
association with the density at which force and shape errors level off, none
survives false-discovery correction, so within these nine gel designs there is
no reason to revisit pixel density when the gel changes. What does move it is
the task and the error tolerance: at a 10 % tolerance the markerless arms
plateau at 5.9-6.3 px/mm^2 for shear and 13.3-17.3 for normal force, with shape
between 14.9 and 55.0, and tightening the tolerance to 5 % raises the
depth-referenced normal-force density more than twelvefold. Choose the gel from
the contact and force requirements; choose the pixel density from the task and
the error you will accept.

================================================================================
I. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

[I-1]
Vision-based tactile sensors (VBTS) observe elastomer deformation to estimate
contact geometry and loading. Their image output supports dense sensing and
image-based learning, but processing multiple tactile streams increases the
demands on a robotic hand's instrumentation. Prior work identifies bandwidth as
a challenge for compact tactile fingers [1]. Input image resolution is
consequently an important design parameter.

[I-2]
The elastomer and optical system transform contact into a spatial image.
Deformation can spread a localized contact, potentially attenuating fine
features before sampling. This motivates the hypothesis that changing elastomer
thickness or hardness also changes the input resolution required for
estimation. However, distinguishing nearby contacts and estimating a net force
are different objectives. A model may exploit broad intensity or deformation
cues even when fine contact details are blurred. Their resolution requirements
need not change in the same way.

[I-3]
Existing resolution sweeps generally use a fixed elastomer [4], [5], [8].
Conversely, material and thickness studies characterize sensitivity or
measurement range without jointly sweeping input resolution [2], [6], [7].
Cross-device benchmarks [3] compare complete sensors, but optics, materials,
and field of view change together. The studies reviewed here therefore leave
the relationship between elastomer design and task-specific input sampling
unresolved.

[I-4]
We examine this relationship using 27 sensors, one per condition. The main
comparison includes depth-referenced and photometric sensors, each with three
hardness levels and sensing-layer thicknesses of 1, 2, and 3 mm. A
marker-bearing photometric variant is retained as a secondary arm. Within each
arm, the camera, lens, illumination, and enclosure are reused while gels are
exchanged. Thickness still changes the optical path, so shared hardware does
not imply identical optical conditions.

[I-5]
We distinguish direct image characterizations from calibrated estimation tasks.
The former measure imprint response, paired-contact separability, and scalar
image-response saturation. The latter measure force and shape errors as a
common capture is downsampled. Acquisition is quasi-static at 4.9 frames per
second. We evaluate processed image resolution, not lower-resolution camera
hardware, link bandwidth, or dynamic control performance.

[I-6]
The selected specimens exhibit different median plateau resolutions across
force components and reconstruction tasks. No tested hardness or thickness
association survives false-discovery correction, but the small design and the
scatter of the per-unit estimates prevent an independence claim. The contributions are:

[I-C1]
1) A physical comparison of elastomer design and input sampling with shared
imaging hardware within each sensor arm.

[I-C2]
2) Task-specific force and shape error measurements, plateau-resolution
summaries, and comparisons of nominally identical specimens.

[I-C3]
3) Image-based characterizations that distinguish paired-contact separability
and criterion-dependent response saturation from estimator performance.

II. RELATED WORK
================================================================================

[II-1]
Andrussow et al. report comparable force and contact-location performance after
reducing input from 410x308 to 82x60 pixels, approximately 1.7 px/mm for their
sensor [4]. They suggest elastomer smoothing and shell design as possible
limits. Shepherd et al. examine resolution in texture classification [5], and
Krohn et al. study tactile resolution in manipulation [8]. These results
motivate input sweeps but do not establish how a sweep changes with the
elastomer.

[II-2]
Halwani et al. investigate sensitivity and measurement range in a simulated
multilayer elastomer [2]. Agarwal et al. explore PDMS thickness and optical
design in simulation [6]. Kakani et al. evaluate elastomer combinations through
force-displacement measurements [7]. These studies address material or geometry
selection without a joint input-resolution sweep.

[II-3]
TacEva evaluates four VBTS configurations under a common framework [3]. Such
comparisons assess complete devices, whereas our within-arm experiment reuses
hardware across specimens. Table I summarizes the factors varied in the
reviewed work.

================================================================================
III. SPECIMENS AND EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
================================================================================

A. Sensor Hardware

[III-A1]
Depth-referenced sensors encode deformation through intensity changes [10],
whereas photometric sensors use directionally colored illumination to encode
surface gradients [9]. The marker-bearing variant shares the photometric
formulations.

[III-A2]
We use three bodies, one per arm, adapted from the corresponding published
designs to accommodate a shared camera module and mounting arrangement. Gels
are exchanged within each body. All bodies use a Sincerefirst SF-YL0320-V1 /
SF-N1050-V1 module with a D140 lens, recording uncompressed 1920x1080 YUYV at
4.9 frames per second. Exposure, white balance, and gamma are fixed manually.

[III-A3]
An acrylic window supports the gel; it is 2 mm thick in the depth-referenced
body and 3 mm in the photometric body. White LEDs illuminate the
depth-referenced stack from below. Photometric bodies use red, green, and blue
illumination from three sides. LED strips are powered by a 3.3 V bench supply
to reduce dependence on USB supply variations.

[III-A4]
Increasing thickness changes camera-to-surface distance and may alter
magnification and focus. A per-unit image-scale estimate is obtained using
known probe geometry, but its uncertainty has not been independently
established. Analysis uses a central image region to reduce peripheral optical
effects. Consequently, this experiment does not isolate mechanical effects from
thickness-dependent optical changes.


B. Elastomer Specimens and Analysis Sets

[III-B1]
The analysed set is 27 specimens, one per condition and nine per arm. Two were
cast for every condition, 54 in all, and were visually screened and checked for
thickness before measurement; one was destroyed during an early force ramp. The
second specimen of each condition exists but is not analysed in this paper, for
the reason given in [III-B2]. Specimens were measured sequentially rather than
in immediate pairs, so any two of them differ by session and mounting as well
as by design.

[III-B2]
Every analysis in this paper uses the same 27 specimens, one per condition and
nine per arm. Earlier versions of some analyses were computed over larger,
unequal sets; those are not reported here, and where an analysis could not be
recomputed on the 27 it has been removed rather than mixed in. One consequence
is stated where it bites: quantities that compare two specimens built to the
same condition are outside this set by construction, so this paper does not
report build-to-build repeatability.

[III-B3]
Selection prioritized the specimen without a confirmed defect (two light-leak
cases), then the specimen with more usable measurements (four cases), then the
sole surviving specimen (one case); otherwise r1 was used (20 conditions).
Selection by usable measurement count can depend on sensor response when
contact detection fails. Its influence requires an analysis including all valid
specimens.

[III-B6]
Mixtures are degassed and cured at room temperature for at least the

The marker pattern is designed as 1 mm dots on 2.5 mm centers. A water-slide

C. Apparatus and Probes

[III-C1]
A FAIRINO FR5 arm presses printed indenters into a sensor mounted above an ATI
Mini45 force/torque transducer. The transducer uses SI-145-5 calibration, with
nominal ranges of 145 N for Fx/Fy, 290 N for Fz, and 5 N m for torques. Signals
are acquired using an NI PCIe-6343 and SCB-68A with the manufacturer's
calibration matrix.

[III-C2]
The table-mounted transducer remains fixed relative to the sensor. A constant
transform to the robot base is measured once. Probe endpoints share a nominal
mounting distance, and the gel plane is re-estimated after mounting. Reported
tool calibration uses z = 36.794 mm at the center of the 4 mm sphere, 2 mm
above its endpoint.

IV. IMAGE-BASED CHARACTERIZATION
================================================================================

A. Imprint Growth and Optical Response

[IV-A1]
Imprint-diameter growth and mean intensity change are fitted against
indentation over each specimen's valid depth interval. Diameter is reported in
pixels. Since image scale and fitted intervals vary, these slopes characterize
the recorded response rather than mechanical spreading alone.

[IV-A2]
Photometric diameter slopes decrease with thickness in each hardness group
(Table IV). Depth-referenced slopes have no consistent ordering across hardness
groups. The comparison combines deformation, image formation, and the interval
used for fitting.

  are fitted over each unit's own valid depth band, so they are not comparable
  across principles either ([IV-A1]).

[IV-A3]
Comparisons at equal force and equal depth probe different properties: at equal
force, compliance changes penetration as well as the image. We therefore
specify the loading variable rather than treating the resulting slopes as
interchangeable.

[IV-A4]
Specimens differ substantially within an arm, by roughly a factor of three in
diameter slope across the nine depth-referenced units and 2.6 across the nine
photometric ones, and that spread is not ordered by hardness. Assembly, optics,
coating, realized thickness, curing, and adhesion were not varied
independently, so the spread cannot be attributed. Because this paper analyses
one specimen per condition, it also cannot separate specimen-to-specimen
variation from the designed factors: a build effect and a gel effect enter the
same numbers. Slopes are additionally affected by contact-detection failure at
the end of each valid interval.


B. Paired-Contact Separation

[IV-B1]
Two 1.00 mm cylinders are pressed with edge gaps of 0.10-1.50 mm, corresponding
to center spacings of 1.10-2.50 mm. Along the axis joining the contacts, the
difference-image profile gives the weaker peak P and intervening valley T. We
compute dip = (P-T)/P.

[IV-B2]
The signal gate requires P >= 2.5 gray levels. A noise gate requires dip to
exceed three times its propagated uncertainty. A Rayleigh-inspired criterion
requires dip >= 0.265; the stricter contrast criterion adopted from Digit 360
[11] requires dip >= 0.667. The latter corresponds to (P-T)/(P+T) >= 0.5 under
the stated peak/valley convention. Sharing this threshold does not establish a
common spatial transfer function or direct comparability with different probe
geometries. A spacing passes if at least one tested depth passes all gates.

[IV-B3]
All nine selected photometric specimens separate two 1 mm cylinders with a
0.10 mm edge gap, with dips of 0.967-1.000. This bounds the distinguishable gap
for this geometry; it is not a general 0.10 mm resolution measurement.

[IV-B4]
Depth-referenced specimens resolve center spacings from 1.10 to 2.50 mm
(Table V), with larger spacings generally required by 3 mm specimens.
Resolved-depth intervals are those observed within the applied ladders, not
necessarily complete operating limits.

[IV-B5]
Over the nine depth-referenced specimens, thickness is associated with every
separation measure we record and hardness with none. The finest resolved centre
spacing rises with thickness (Spearman rho = +0.75, p = 0.021), while the
maximum dip falls (rho = -0.74, p = 0.023) and so does the number of gaps
resolved (rho = -0.71, p = 0.031); against hardness the same three give
rho = -0.13, -0.32 and +0.22, none significant. In the photometric arm the
finest resolved spacing and the gap count are constant at the bound of our
probe set, so only the maximum dip varies, and it shows no thickness
association (rho = +0.16, p = 0.688). These are three correlations on nine
units each, reported outside the false-discovery family of Section V, and they
concern paired-contact image contrast rather than an isolated elastomer
transfer function.

[IV-B6]
The result depends on indentation and threshold: shallow contacts may be too
weak, while deeper contacts may merge. Lowering the signal gate from 2.5 to 2.0
gray levels increases the reported count resolving 1.25 mm center spacing from
five to nine. The marker arm is not evaluated, and each depth uses one frame,
leaving within-depth repeatability unquantified.


C. Image-Response Saturation

[IV-C1]
We define an operational threshold-crossing force from a scalar image-response
slope. Reduced sensitivity of this statistic does not imply that the complete
image contains no additional force information.

[IV-C2]
An 8 mm sphere advances in steps. The statistic is mean absolute image
difference from an undeformed reference over the central half of the frame. Its
force derivative is fitted over three successive steps. The reported force is
the first of two steps below a selected slope threshold. During acquisition,
stopping conditions, including the nominal 0.9-thickness depth backstop, were
inhibited below 10 N. The absolute_ceiling.py post-processing accepts samples
at force >= 0.3 N, first requires the response to exceed the threshold, and
then seeks two sub-threshold hits. Post-processed crossings can therefore occur
below the 10 N acquisition stopping force.

[IV-C3]
The relative criterion uses 15 % of a running robust peak response, implemented
as the median of the three largest eligible slopes. Because the threshold is a
fraction of each specimen's own peak, a specimen with a weak response reports a
higher crossing force, so the criterion states an operating limit for one
specimen and does not compare specimens on a common scale. A fixed gray
levels/N threshold would avoid that, but it is not normalized by each arm's
image noise and we have not computed it on this specimen set. Neither is a
universal sensor limit.

Median crossing force is higher in the depth-referenced arm, and the two groups

[IV-C6]
Under the relative criterion, thickness is positively associated with crossing
force in the depth-referenced arm (rho = 0.63, p = 0.068), negatively
associated in the photometric arm (rho = -0.79, p = 0.011), and has rho = -0.53,
p = 0.145 in the marker arm. These descriptive trends do not establish opposite
physical mechanisms.

[IV-C7]
Recorded crossing displacement increases with nominal thickness in all arms
(rho = 0.90-0.95, p < 0.001); no hardness association is detected (rho = -0.05
to 0.11, p > 0.5). Values are 4.3-6.8 mm and 1.1-2.1 mm in the depth-referenced
and photometric arms. Total stack thickness, contact-zero uncertainty, actual
indentation, and fixture compliance have not been jointly quantified. Dividing
this displacement by nominal sensing-layer thickness therefore cannot establish
backing contact.

[IV-C8]
Within the depth-referenced arm, the hard-to-soft crossing-force ratio is 0.99,
1.43, and 2.04 at 1, 2, and 3 mm. This suggests a possible interaction but does
not establish a design rule.

[IV-C9]
Marker specimens use a 4 mm sphere, so their 6.2-20.5 N range and 9.2 N median
are not directly comparable to the markerless ramps. Across the nine specimens
in this set tested with both sphere sizes, the force ratio ranges from 1.50 to
3.04 with a median of 2.42, while the displacement ratio spans 0.83 to 1.12
with a median of 0.99. Displacement transfers between the two probes and force
does not, but nine pairs neither establish probe invariance nor exclude
field-of-view effects.

================================================================================
V. TASK PERFORMANCE AGAINST INPUT RESOLUTION
================================================================================

A. Force Estimation

[V-A1]
A ResNet-18 is trained separately for each specimen and each of twelve input
resolutions, obtained by downsampling 1920x1080 captures to widths from 1920 to
8 pixels. Each specimen contributes 1000 frames. Labels are wrench averages
over the corresponding exposure window, and data are split by loading cycle.
The quasi-static protocol spans approximately 0-2.1 N normal force and shear up
to 0.5 N.

We report the sampling requirement as pixel density R in px/mm^2 rather than
pixel width, because the three arms image different physical areas: R is the
pixel count at a given downscale divided by the field-of-view area measured in
that unit's scale calibration, which reduces to (s*k)^2 for a native scale s in
px/mm and a downscale factor k = width/1920. The height term cancels because
the aspect ratio is preserved. Native scales come from the per-unit calibration
for the two markerless arms and from the marker lattice pitch for the marker
arm, whose regression-based scale is 20 % low.

[V-A2]
The model is an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 with a six-output head. Training
runs for 30 epochs with Adam (learning rate 5e-4, weight decay 1e-4), linear
learning-rate decay to 0.1 of its initial value, and three seeds. Targets are
min-max scaled using the training set, and the L1 loss is summed. The
checkpoint with lowest validation loss is restored before test evaluation.
Cycle boundaries target 70/15/15 % train/validation/test proportions separately
in normal and shear blocks, preferring at least 8 % test data where feasible.
Resizing uses OpenCV INTER_AREA on the full frame.

[V-A3]
The optimizer batch is 64 at every width. The memory allowance is rounded down
to a divisor of the batch, giving micro-batch/accumulation pairs of 4x16 at
1920 pixels, 8x8 at 1280, 16x4 at 854, 32x2 at 640, and 64x1 at 426 and below.

An earlier version rounded the accumulation count instead, which left three of
the twelve widths optimized at effective batches of 66, 72 and 44 rather than
64 -- the fault the accumulation was introduced to remove. Those three widths
were retrained for all three arms after the fix and the corrected values are
the ones reported here. The correction moved the affected medians by -8 % to
+12 % with no consistent sign, and changed none of the conclusions: no gel-term
comparison reaches q < 0.05 either before or after, and the marker arm's shear
trend is unchanged. Median plateau widths fell by one or two ladder steps.

BatchNorm remains in training mode and uses micro-batch statistics, so the
normalization statistics still differ across widths even though the optimizer
step no longer does.

[V-A4]
Depth-referenced inputs encode reference, brightening, and darkening channels.
Photometric inputs use the camera frame. Marker-bearing inputs retain the
grayscale reference channel and fill masked locations in the signed difference
image before constructing brightening/darkening channels. This suppresses the
selected displacement cues but does not remove every trace of markers. It is an
inpainted-difference representation, not a validation of explicit marker
tracking. Cross-arm comparisons include preprocessing, material, and hardware
differences.

[V-A5]
We define the plateau resolution as the lowest tested width whose error is at
most 1.10 times the specimen's minimum across widths. This is a descriptive
near-minimum point, not necessarily the start of a monotonic plateau. It
depends on grid spacing, training variability, and tolerance. The exported
force summaries use all-test-frame axis MAEs; shear is the mean of Fx and Fy
MAEs, not lateral-magnitude MAE restricted to shear cycles. The plateau is
selected from test-error summaries and should not be treated as an
independently validated deployment choice. We recomputed this statistic from
the exported per-seed CSVs at 5 %, 10 %, and 20 % tolerance, first taking the
seed median per specimen and width and then the median specimen plateau.
Results appear below.

[V-A7]
Twelve force comparisons test plateau-resolution associations with thickness
and hardness within each arm and axis group. Together with eight shape
comparisons, they undergo one false-discovery correction; none reaches
q < 0.05. These comparisons share specimens and outputs. Rank correlations at
three levels do not adequately characterize interactions or arbitrary
nonmonotonic effects, and the result does not establish independence.

[V-A8]
The per-unit plateau densities behind Table IX are widely spread within each
arm -- 1.7 to 880 px/mm^2 for depth-referenced Fz (32x18 to 640x360), and 16.5
to 5898 for marker-bearing shear (80x45 to 1280x720) -- so the medians
summarize a scattered quantity rather than a repeatable one. Because this paper analyses one specimen per condition,
we cannot say how much of that spread is build-to-build variation and how much
is the designed factors, and we do not report a repeatability figure. That
missing measurement is the main reason the negative result below is stated as a
failure to detect.

At 5 % tolerance, photometric shear has a higher median plateau density than
Fz (85.8 against 17.4 px/mm^2); at 10 % the ordering reverses (6.3 against
13.3), and the depth-referenced arm falls from 218 to 17 px/mm^2 across the
same step, a factor of thirteen. Thus neither the normal/shear ordering nor the
absolute density is robust to tolerance choice. A fixed density recommendation
for shear is not supported independently of an allowed-error criterion.

[V-A10]
The exported CSVs already contain constant training-mean baselines for Fz and
lateral magnitude. Median Fz baseline errors are approximately 0.288, 0.305,
and 0.443 N in the three selected arms. The learned estimates improve
substantially on these baselines, although the baseline for lateral magnitude
is not directly comparable to the mean of Fx/Fy MAEs. Contact-only and
load-stratified analyses remain necessary for stronger task claims.



B. Shape Reconstruction

[V-B1]
Depth-referenced reconstruction uses a brightness-to-depth lookup table
calibrated on the 4 mm sphere and evaluated on the held-out cylinder and cube.
Photometric reconstruction fits an MLP from color differences and normalized
pixel position to surface gradients, using spherical Hertz-contact gradients as
model-based calibration targets. A discrete-cosine-transform Poisson solver
integrates predicted gradients. Both pipelines are fitted to data; marker
specimens are excluded. In the documented depth-referenced sweep, contact
centers are detected at native resolution and rescaled, isolating
reconstruction under reduced sampling from low-resolution contact localization.
Thus it is not an entirely low-resolution end-to-end pipeline.

[V-B2]
For depth-referenced reconstruction, median absolute error falls from 0.529 mm
at 8x5 pixels to 0.062 mm at 160x90, then increases. A correction to
lookup-table gray-level coverage flattens the curve above 80 pixels of width.
Plateau estimates therefore depend on the reconstruction implementation as well
as sampling. The repository's knee_by_cell.py uses the uncorrected cylinder and
cube errors for the depth-referenced plateau, so Table XI summarizes the
uncorrected reconstruction. The exact gray-level coverage correction must still
be described if corrected curves are included.

[V-B3]
Photometric reconstruction uses a noncontact reference recorded with the
indenter positioned above the gel. References taken before approach were
8-14 % brighter than subsequent frames. Selecting the positioned reference
avoids this offset without rescaling the images.

[V-B4]
Over the nine photometric specimens, the median best error is 0.048 mm for the
cylinder and 0.055 mm for the cube, with eight of nine below 0.1 mm on each
probe; the depth-referenced medians are 0.062 and 0.093 mm, with seven and five
of nine below 0.1 mm. Evaluation uses commanded penetration relative to a
fitted contact zero over the contact region; neither held-out probe enters
calibration. Absolute errors are not used to rank the arms, for the reason
given in [V-B7].

[V-B5]
The eight shape comparisons detect no hardness or thickness association after
correction. Their agreement with the force analysis remains exploratory because
both use a small specimen design and calibrated pipelines.

[V-B6]
In the depth-referenced lookup-table encoding, one gray level corresponds to
31-64 micrometers of depth across nine specimens, a 2.1-fold range. This is an
effective encoding step rather than a universal depth-accuracy bound; spatial
inference need not share it.

[V-B7]
Shape evaluation has unresolved uncertainty. Contact-zero repeatability and
commanded-to-actual displacement error are unquantified. Photometric
calibration uses a half-space model for finite layered specimens. Recovered
depth is approximately 0.47 of the reference in the reported comparison, and
cross-specimen depth slopes range from 0.16 to 0.92. High within-specimen
correlation therefore does not establish correct depth scale. A sphere-derived
scale correction increases flat-probe error from 0.042 to 0.151 mm and is not
adopted. Finally, the photometric evaluation spans 0.010-0.578 mm, whereas
depth-referenced evaluation uses a deeper range; absolute errors are not used
to rank principles.


================================================================================
VI. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

[VI-1]
We compared elastomer design and processed image resolution using 27 sensors,
one per condition. Paired-contact contrast degrades with thickness in the
tested depth-referenced configuration, while no tested hardness or thickness
association with estimator plateau resolution survives false-discovery
correction.

[VI-2]
At a 10 % tolerance, markerless specimens have median plateau densities of
5.9-6.3 px/mm^2 for shear and 13.3-17.3 for normal force; shape summaries range
from 14.9 to 55.0. Tightening the tolerance to 5 % raises the depth-referenced
normal-force density more than twelvefold and reverses the normal/shear
ordering in the photometric arm, so these densities are observations about a
criterion and a pipeline rather than sensor specifications. A small design,
scattered per-unit plateau estimates, unmeasured build-to-build repeatability
and unquantified measurement uncertainty together prevent an independence claim
or a universal input-resolution recommendation.

[VI-3]
The practical implication is that the two decisions can be taken separately.
Choose the gel from what the contact requires: paired-contact separation, the
force at which the image response saturates, and the imprint response, all
three of which move with thickness in our measurements. Choose the pixel
density from the task and the error tolerance, and, within the nine gel designs
tested, do not revisit it when the gel changes. Extending that guidance beyond
these designs requires the second specimen of each condition analysed as a
repeatability study, effect sizes with intervals, and validation over the
relevant load and motion range.

EDITORIAL DECISIONS CARRIED IN

- Image-response saturation is demoted to a supporting result. Restoring it as
  a headline needs the absolute analysis on the same specimen set.
- The marker arm inpaints the signal, so it is not presented as validation of
  marker-displacement sensing.
- Equivalence testing is needed only if an independence claim is revived. For
  an exploratory result, effect size and uncertainty come first.
- Scope-widening phrasing ("first", "the two dominant principles", "a limit no
  estimator can pass") has been reduced.
- Section order III-V is kept so the tables still line up. Placing the task
  results first is a reasonable alternative at typesetting time.
