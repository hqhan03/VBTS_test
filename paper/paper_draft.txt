ABSTRACT
================================================================================

[ABS]
Abstract-- Vision-based tactile sensors encode contact as an image, and the
sampling resolution at which that image is processed fixes the camera, the link
bandwidth and the network input size of an entire finger. The elastomer is
widely assumed to set that requirement: a thicker or stiffer layer spreads an
indentation and attenuates fine detail before the camera sees it, so the
resolution a task needs should move with the gel. This has been conjectured but
never measured, because measuring it means varying the elastomer while holding
the optics fixed. We fabricate 27 sensors spanning the two dominant
intensity-based sensing principles, depth-referenced and photometric-stereo,
each crossed with three elastomer hardness levels and three thicknesses (1, 2,
3 mm), with one body, camera and illumination per principle; a marker-bearing
variant of the photometric gel is built on the same grid as an additional arm.
Each unit is evaluated on three calibration-free gel characterizations and on
two estimation tasks swept against sampling resolution, for which we re-derive
the preprocessing rather than inherit a pipeline built for a different sensor.
Training-free measurements confirm that the elastomer does filter spatially:
imprint bandwidth and optical blur both shift monotonically with thickness.
[X: whether that shift reaches the resolution at which a trained estimator
saturates -- the central question -- is reported from the re-derived sweep.]
What the elastomer separately controls is clearer: the maximum force readable
from the image and the depth quantization of shape reconstruction vary [Y]-fold
across these gels.

I. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

[I-1]
Vision-based tactile sensors (VBTS) encode contact into an image. A camera
observes the deformation of a soft elastomer, so a single frame carries dense
information about contact geometry, normal and shear loading, and surface
detail. The representation is also convenient for learning, since the sensor
output is already the input format that modern vision architectures expect.

[I-2]
The cost is data volume. Each sensor produces an image stream rather than a
handful of scalar channels, and that cost multiplies with the number of contact
points on a hand. Aggregate bandwidth, transfer latency, and inference time
begin to consume the control budget available for reactive manipulation;
high-bandwidth instrumentation has been identified as a fundamental obstacle to
integrating VBTS into compact fingers [1], and recent multi-fingered systems
list latency minimization among their primary contributions. The sampling
resolution -- pixels per millimetre of sensing surface actually retained and
processed -- must therefore be fixed early, because it propagates into the
camera module, the transfer and compute electronics, and the network input
size.

[I-3]
The natural expectation is that this choice depends on the gel. The camera
never observes the contact directly, only the contact as rendered by the
elastomer, and the elastomer acts as a spatial low-pass filter: a thinner or
softer layer conforms more locally and preserves fine detail, while a thicker
or stiffer one distributes an indentation over a wider area and attenuates high
spatial frequencies before the camera sees them. If that is true at the scale
that matters, then resolution cannot be specified independently of gel design,
and every published saturation point -- three-axis force and location
estimation saturating near 1.7 px/mm [4], texture classification at roughly a
quarter of the original image [5] -- is a statement about one particular gel
rather than about the task.

[I-4]
No existing study can settle this, because each varies one side of the
relation. Studies that sweep camera resolution hold the elastomer fixed [4],
[5], [8]. Studies that vary elastomer modulus or thickness characterize
sensitivity and measurement range but treat resolution as given [2], [7], or
explore the design space in simulation [6]. Comparative frameworks span several
commercial devices [3], where optics, field of view, and gel formulation differ
simultaneously and no single variable can be isolated.

[I-5]
This paper measures both sides together. We fabricate 27 sensors. The main
design spans the two dominant intensity-based sensing principles --
depth-referenced [10] and photometric-stereo [9] -- each crossed with three
elastomer hardness levels and three thicknesses (1, 2, 3 mm). A marker-bearing
variant of the photometric gel is built on the same grid and carried as an
additional arm rather than as a third level, since it shares the photometric
formulations and is not measured on every test. Within a principle the sensor body, camera,
lens, illumination and supply voltage are identical across all gels; the
optical distance from the camera to the sensing surface necessarily moves with
gel thickness, so image scale is measured on every unit rather than assumed.
Units are measured in a shuffled order rather than blocked by thickness, so
that anything drifting over the campaign cannot arrive in the results wearing
the costume of a design effect. Resolution is varied in post-processing by
downscaling a common 1920x1080 capture, so no hardware change is confounded
with the sweep. Each unit is evaluated on five tests, grouped by whether they
require fitting to data. Three characterize the gel directly: the ratio by
which the imprint of a spherical indenter reads wider in the image than the
contact geometrically is, two-point spatial resolution with paired-cylinder
indenters, and the maximum force still readable from the image. Two are
estimation tasks fitted to each unit's own measurements: 6-axis force
estimation under a robot-driven normal and shear protocol with ground truth
from a table-mounted F/T sensor, and depth-from-intensity shape reconstruction
against probes of known geometry.

[I-6]
The saturation resolution does not move with the gel. Across nine gel designs
and two independent principles, force-estimation error saturates at an input
width of roughly 40-56 px over a ~19 mm field, about 2.5 px/mm, with no
significant dependence on either thickness or hardness. We report this as a
negative result deliberately: an earlier, coarser sweep in this same campaign
produced a significant thickness correlation that did not survive a finer
resolution ladder or a change of input representation, and we show why an
unstable knee estimate manufactured it. The underlying physics is present but
small -- imprint bandwidth and optical blur both shift monotonically with
thickness in training-free measurements -- and simply does not reach the scale
at which a trained estimator's requirement changes.

[I-7]
The practical consequence is favourable rather than disappointing. Sampling
resolution for force estimation can be specified before the elastomer is
chosen, which decouples two design decisions that were assumed to be entangled.
What the elastomer does control is different: the maximum force readable from
the image, and the depth quantization of shape reconstruction, both of which
vary severalfold across our gels. The contributions are:

[I-C1]
1) A controlled, hardware-matched factorial study of elastomer hardness,
elastomer thickness, sensing principle, and sampling resolution on physically
fabricated sensors -- to our knowledge the first in which all four are varied
together with optics held fixed.

[I-C2]
2) The measured saturation resolution for force estimation, ~2.5 px/mm, shown
to be invariant to gel design across two sensing principles, together with the
evidence that an apparent gel dependence in a coarser experiment was an
artefact of the knee estimator.

[I-C3]
3) Measured gel dependence where it does exist: force ceiling under an absolute
image-response criterion, and depth quantization for shape reconstruction.

[I-C4]
4) A fabrication guideline that separates what must be co-designed from what
need not be, and an explicit account of which claims our design cannot support.

[I-8]
Section II reviews prior work. Section III describes fabrication and the design
of experiments. Section IV reports the three characterizations that need no
fitting to data -- imprint spread ratio, two-point spatial resolution, and
maximum measurable force -- and so speak about the gel itself. Section V
reports the two estimation tasks, force estimation and shape reconstruction,
each swept against sampling resolution. Section VI concludes.

II. RELATED WORK
================================================================================

[II-1]
Two families of VBTS design recur in this paper. Depth-referenced sensors place
an opaque layer over a backlit gel and read indentation depth from how much the
layer darkens, so a single grey image maps monotonically to height [10].
Photometric-stereo sensors illuminate the gel from several directions with
coloured light and recover surface gradient from the colour of the reflection
[9], optionally with printed markers whose motion carries shear. The two
families differ in what the camera is asked to resolve -- an intensity field in
one case, a gradient field in the other -- which is why we treat them as
separate axes rather than pooling them.

[II-2]
*Resolution requirements measured at one design point.* Andrussow et al. sweep
the input to their fingertip sensor from 410x308 down to 4x3 and report that
direct force and contact-location prediction is essentially unchanged at 82x60
px -- about 1.7 px/mm over a 1740 mm^2 sensing surface -- while cutting the
pixel count by a factor of 25 [4]. Shepherd et al. report texture
classification saturating near a quarter of the original image [5], and Krohn
et al. relate tactile resolution to manipulation performance at the taxel level
[8]. Each result comes from a single elastomer. Andrussow et al. go further and
name the reason: they conclude that the sensor's spatial resolution is limited
neither by input image resolution nor by network capacity nor by test-bed
accuracy, and conjecture that the physical smoothing properties of the
elastomer and the shell design are what bounds it [4]. That conjecture is the
hypothesis of the present paper. It has never been tested, because testing it
requires varying the elastomer, which a single-sensor study cannot do.

[II-3]
*Elastomer studies that hold resolution fixed.* Halwani et al. vary modulus in
a two-layer elastomer (1.75 and 2.5 mm) and report sensitivity and measurement
range, with sensitivity defined as force per marker displacement, in simulation
[2]. Agarwal et al. sweep surface curvature, illumination, and coating material
over 1-3 mm PDMS layers, also in simulation [6]. Kakani et al. select an
elastomer combination over Shore OO 40-80 and 1.0-2.5 mm from measured
force-displacement behaviour [7]. All three treat the camera as given: none
reports what happens when pixels are removed.

[II-4]
*Evaluation frameworks across devices.* Cong et al. benchmark four VBTS with
distinct sensing mechanisms -- ViTacTip, MagicTac, and marker and markerless
GelSight Mini variants -- on a common pipeline spanning intrinsic, performance,
and robustness metrics [3]. Camera resolution, gel thickness, and field of view
appear there as catalogued intrinsic attributes of each device rather than as
controlled variables: optics, illumination, and gel formulation move together
from one device to the next, so no single factor can be isolated. A controlled
answer requires fabricating the specimens, which is what we do.

[II-5]
Table I places these together. The pattern is that resolution and elastomer
design have each been varied, and never in the same experiment.

[II-6]
*Practice in learned tactile force estimation.* The gap also shows in how these
models are trained. Sparsh resizes its two-frame tactile input to 224x224 for
ViT processing and states that ablating the effect of input image and patch
resolution is left for future work [12]. FeelAnyForce pads its 240x320 GelSight
Mini images to 320x320 and rescales them to 224x224, and ablates the encoder
and the depth-reconstruction head but not the input size [13]. FEATS does vary
resolution, but on the output side: it compares U-net force-distribution
outputs of 12x16 and 24x32 and keeps the latter for spatial detail [14]. Input
sampling resolution is therefore inherited rather than measured, and the one
resolution ablation in this group concerns what the network emits rather than
what it is given. Our result partly justifies the 224 px convention and partly
does not: shear error is lowest in a band that contains it, while normal force
is already flat far below, so 224 px is generous for applications that need
only normal force.

[II-7]
*Measurement conventions we adopt.* For two-point spatial resolution we report
both the classical Rayleigh dip and the Michelson-contrast criterion used by
Digit 360 with a dual-pronged microindenter [11], since the latter makes our
numbers directly comparable to theirs and is the stricter of the two. Both
criteria are fixed before any paired-probe data is examined.

III. SENSOR FABRICATION AND DESIGN OF EXPERIMENTS
================================================================================

A. Sensing Principles and Sensor Bodies

[III-A1]
The study covers the two dominant intensity-based VBTS principles -- one
depth-referenced, one photometric-stereo -- with a marker-bearing variant of
the photometric gel carried alongside. Markers are not treated as a third
principle: those units use the photometric formulations unchanged, and they are
not measured on every test, so they are reported as an additional arm wherever
the data supports it.

[III-A1b]
We build one sensor body per sensing principle and swap gels within it, so that
every gel of a principle is read by the same camera, the same lens, the same
illumination and the same enclosure. The three bodies were designed by us,
following the published open designs of the corresponding sensors as closely as
the shared camera and mount allowed. Using our own bodies rather than
off-the-shelf units is what makes the comparison controlled: a commercial
device fixes its gel, and a study that swaps devices necessarily swaps optics
and illumination at the same time (Section II-D).

[III-A2]
All three bodies carry the same camera, a Sincerefirst SF-YL0320-V1 /
SF-N1050-V1 module fitted with a D140 lens, streaming uncompressed 1920x1080
YUYV at a measured 4.9 frames per second. Exposure, white balance and gamma are
set manually and held fixed for the whole campaign, so that a change in the
image is a change in the contact and not in the camera's own adaptation.

[III-A3]
In every body an acrylic window sits directly above the camera, supporting the
gel and keeping it from touching the lens. The window is 2 mm thick in the
depth-referenced body and 3 mm in the photometric one.

[III-A4]
Illumination differs by principle because the two sensing mechanisms require
it. The depth-referenced body is lit from below by a white LED strip that
shines up through the gel, so the image is a transmission map and indentation
appears as darkening. The photometric-stereo bodies are lit from three sides by
an LED strip supplying red, green and blue, so surface gradient appears as a
shift in colour. In both, the strips are driven at a constant 3.3 V from a bench
power supply rather than from the USB rail, so brightness does not drift with
load over a session or between units.

[III-A5]
Two consequences of this arrangement should be stated rather than glossed.
First, the optical distance from the camera to the sensing surface is not the
same across gels: a 3 mm gel places its contact surface further from the camera
than a 1 mm gel does. This is not an uncontrolled nuisance but a direct
consequence of the design variable itself, and it is why image scale (mm per
pixel) is measured on every unit with the robot rather than taken from a
nominal figure. Second, all analysis is restricted to a central region of the
frame and excludes the sensor periphery, so vignetting and the oblique view at
the edge of a wide-angle lens do not enter any measurement.


B. Elastomer Specimens

[III-B1]
The design is a full factorial of three sensing principles, three hardness
levels and three thicknesses. Table II gives the formulation of each gel.
Several sensors were cast for every condition; each was screened by visual
inspection and a thickness check before any measurement, so the screening
selects on fabrication quality and not on outcome. Two replicates of every cell
were then kept and measured, 36 units in all, and every result in this paper is
reported on one replicate per cell -- the 27 units of the title -- chosen by the
rule below. The second replicate is not discarded: it is what tells us how far
two nominally identical units disagree, which Section IV-C needs in order to
interpret its own criterion.

[III-B1b]
*Choosing one replicate per cell.* The rule is fixed on evidence independent of
the measured outcome and applied in order. (i) If one replicate has a confirmed
hardware defect, keep the other; two depth-referenced units had a visually
confirmed light leak. (ii) If one replicate has independent evidence of
under-measurement -- fewer usable data points than its twin, which is a
statement about how much was measured rather than about the value measured --
keep the other; four units qualified. (iii) If only one replicate exists, keep
it; one depth-referenced gel was destroyed by an early ramp. (iv) Otherwise
keep r1. The fourth case covers 20 of the 27 cells and is arbitrary on purpose.
We tested the obvious alternative -- voting across fourteen metrics for
whichever replicate sits closer to the cell expectation -- against the two cells
whose answer is already known from the confirmed light leaks: it identified one
and got the other backwards. It is also biased in the direction that matters,
since keeping the replicate nearer the trend shrinks the scatter and inflates
the effect being measured. An arbitrary rule is unbiased with respect to the
trend; a rule that looks defensible is not.

  Depth-referenced | soft   | OO-30  | Ecoflex 00-30 A : B = 1 : 1
  Depth-referenced | medium | OO-50  | Ecoflex 00-50 A : B = 1 : 1
  Depth-referenced | hard   | OO-70  | Dragon Skin 20 A : B :

  Marker units use the same three photometric formulations.

[III-B3]
Hardness was measured with a Shore 00 durometer on separate flat specimens cast
at least 20 mm thick from the same batches, rather than on the sensor gels
themselves, so that the reading is a property of the material and not of the
backing beneath a 1 mm layer.

[III-B4]
*The two hardness scales are not the same ruler, and this bounds what can be
claimed.* The depth-referenced series spans OO-30 to OO-70, forty durometer
points, and crosses two product families. The photometric series spans OO-51 to
OO-57, six points, obtained by changing only the plasticizer fraction of a
single formulation. The depth-referenced series therefore varies hardness 6.7
times more widely than the photometric one. Two things follow. A soft gel of
one principle is not the counterpart of a soft gel of the other, so hardness is
never treated as a single factor across principles. And a null hardness effect
within the photometric series cannot be distinguished from the restricted range
that produced it; we report it as such rather than as insensitivity to
hardness.

[III-B5]
Gels are cast in silicone moulds made to the target gel height. The quoted
thickness of 1, 2 or 3 mm refers to the sensing layer alone -- the translucent
layer of the depth-referenced design and the transparent layer of the
photometric design -- which is the layer that deforms under contact and creates
the image. The outer optical layer described below sits on top of it and is not
included in that figure. Following the original design, the depth-referenced
stack also carries a transparent Solaris layer beneath the translucent one,
between it and the acrylic window; that layer is held the same across all
units, so the varied hardness and thickness belong to the sensing layer alone.
[CONFIRM: its thickness and formulation]

[III-B5b]
All mixtures are degassed before casting and cured at room temperature for at
least the manufacturer's recommended time. At least five days pass between
casting and measurement, so the material properties have settled before any
unit is loaded.

[III-B6]
*Outer optical layers.* Following the original designs, the depth-referenced
gel carries an outermost black layer, cast 0.5 mm thick from Ecoflex 00-10 with
3 % black pigment, which blocks ambient light and makes the transmitted
intensity a function of indentation alone. The photometric gel carries an
outermost reflective layer of Ecoflex 00-10 with 3 % white pigment, diluted
four-fold in NOVOCS Matte and sprayed on to obtain a uniform coating.

[III-B7]
*Marker grid.* The marker units carry a lattice of 1 mm dots on 2.5 mm centres.
The pattern is printed on water-slide decal paper, and transferred during
casting rather than applied to a finished surface: the gel is first cast in a
mould 0.5 mm below its final height and cured, the decal film is laid on that
surface, a mould at the full gel height is then fitted, and the remaining
silicone is poured and cured over it. The markers therefore sit 0.5 mm below
the outer surface, embedded in the gel rather than printed on it.


C. Apparatus

[III-C1]
A six-axis FAIRINO FR5 arm presses an indenter into the sensor, which is held
in a printed fixture on top of the force/torque transducer. The transducer is
an ATI Mini45 with the SI-145-5 calibration (Fx, Fy to 145 N, Fz to 290 N, Tx,
Ty, Tz to 5 N m), read through an NI PCIe-6343 with an SCB-68A terminal block
on six differential strain-gauge channels, using the manufacturer's calibration
matrix unmodified.

[III-C2]
The transducer is mounted on the table, not on the robot flange. This keeps the
sensor and the wrench origin rigidly fixed to one another for the whole
campaign, at the cost of requiring a constant transform between the sensor
frame and the robot base frame, which is measured once.

[III-C3]
The indenter is a single printed part, and all tips share one tool offset:
tool 1 at z = 36.794 mm, calibrated once and not re-measured per probe. Every
tip is designed so that its endpoint -- the pole of a sphere, the centre of a
cube's end face, the centre of a cylinder's end face -- lies at the same flange
distance, so a surface height found with one probe stays valid for the others.
The offset was calibrated to the centre of the 4 mm sphere, 2 mm above that
common endpoint, and only a calculation that needs a sphere centre (a Hertzian
contact radius, say) adds that offset back.


D. Indenter Probes

[III-D1]
Ten printed tips cover the four tests. A flat-ended cylinder pins the depth
zero and doubles as the image-scale reference, since its imprint is a circle of
known 4.00 mm diameter. A 4 mm sphere calibrates the shape reconstruction and
is deliberately not used to score it; the cube, the cylinder and the star are
the held-out shapes. Paired cylinders of fixed 1.00 mm diameter and varying gap
measure two-point resolution. An 8 mm sphere carries force estimation and the
saturation ramp.

[III-D3]
The probe is the outer loop: one tip is fitted and carried across every sensor
before the next tip is mounted. Looping the other way would require re-fitting
a probe several hundred times, and re-fitting introduces a contact asymmetry of
about 0.09 mm and a height shift of about 0.07 mm -- errors that would land
directly on top of the effects being compared. The gel plane, by contrast, is
re-measured at every mounting.


E. Running Order

[III-E1]
Within a principle the units are measured in a shuffled order fixed by a seed,
so the sequence is reproducible and a paused campaign resumes where it left
off. Thickness is deliberately not blocked. Measuring all the thin gels on one
day and all the thick ones on another would save handling time, but anything
that drifted between those days would then arrive in the results wearing the
costume of a thickness effect, which is one of the two variables the study
exists to measure.

A. Imprint Spread Ratio

B. Two-Point Spatial Resolution

[IV-B1]
Two cylinders of 1.00 mm diameter are pressed into the gel side by side, and we
find the narrowest spacing at which the image still shows two imprints rather
than one. Six probes span edge gaps of 0.10 to 1.50 mm, that is, centre
spacings of 1.10 to 2.50 mm. The method follows the dual-pronged microindenter
of Digit 360 [11] so that our numbers are comparable to theirs.

[IV-B2]
Along the axis joining the two imprints we take the intensity profile of the
difference from the undeformed reference, and from its two peaks and the valley
between them compute dip = (P - T)/P with P the weaker of the two peaks. Using
the weaker peak only makes the test stricter: two points are resolved when both
of them stand as peaks, and averaging would let one deep imprint carry a
shallow one. Three gates must all pass. The signal gate requires P >= 2.5 grey
levels, which is about twice the standard deviation of our depth zero, so that
a peak is not noise. The noise gate requires the dip to exceed three times its
own propagated uncertainty. The resolution gate requires dip >= 0.265, the
Rayleigh criterion. We also report the Michelson-contrast criterion of Digit
360, MTF >= 0.5, which converts to dip >= 0.667 and is therefore 2.5 times
stricter. Both criteria and all three gates were fixed before any paired-probe
data was examined. A unit resolves a spacing if any depth in its ladder passes.

[IV-B3]
Every photometric unit resolves the narrowest probe we built. All nine pass at
a centre spacing of 1.10 mm -- a 0.10 mm gap -- with dips of 0.967 to 1.000,
which clears not only Rayleigh but the Digit 360 criterion by a wide margin.
This is a bound and not a value: what we can state is that the photometric
resolution is finer than 0.10 mm, and the experiment does not say how much
finer. The measurement that does separate these units is the depth over which
they hold that resolution, which is what the right half of Table IV reports.

[IV-B4]
The depth-referenced units do have a measurable limit and it moves with the
gel. The finest resolved spacing ranges from 1.10 mm to 2.50 mm across the nine
units, and the two 3 mm units that were tested at every gap fail everything
below 2.50 mm while the 1 mm and 2 mm units reach 1.10 to 1.75 mm. Resolved
depth behaves in the opposite direction: 1 mm gels hold resolution only to
0.6 mm of indentation, while 2 and 3 mm gels hold it to 0.9 mm.

[IV-B5]
The elastomer does filter spatially, and the cleanest evidence is at the widest
gap. At a centre spacing of 2.50 mm, where the entire 0.1 to 0.9 mm depth
ladder is measurable on every unit, hardness and thickness both order the
depth-referenced results: softer gels separate the two points with a deeper
valley (Spearman rho -0.82 against hardness, p 0.0004), thicker gels are worse
on every metric (rho -0.69 for the count of steps passing the Digit 360
criterion, p 0.0068; rho -0.54 for the maximum dip, p 0.0449), and thicker gels
need a deeper press before the imprint clears the signal gate at all (rho
+0.65, p 0.0127). Steps that pass the Digit 360 criterion occur only in 1 and
2 mm gels; all five 3 mm units score zero. This is the training-free
confirmation that the low-pass argument of Section I is real.

[IV-B6]
It matters that this appears at the widest gap and not the narrowest, because
the narrow-gap pattern is partly a property of the measurement. At 1.10 to
2.00 mm the signal gate removes the shallow steps of a unit and merging removes
the deep ones, so which steps survive differs from unit to unit and the
resolved-or-not pattern carries the measurement as much as the gel. At 2.50 mm
the whole ladder is available on every unit, so the gels are compared on one
ruler. A resolution study that reports only the limiting spacing is therefore
reporting a quantity whose gel dependence it cannot see.

[IV-B7]
Two readings we made along the way were wrong, and both corrections are
instructive. Five depth-referenced units resolved nothing at 2.00 mm or below,
which we first attributed to the body and assembly; at 2.50 mm four of the five
resolve cleanly. Their imprints need 0.3 to 0.5 mm of indentation to clear the
2.5-level signal gate, and the depth ladder of that earlier pass stopped at
0.3 mm, so they were never pressed far enough. The cause was measurement range,
not fabrication. The fifth, a unit with a confirmed light leak, fails at 2.50 mm
too and is genuinely faulty; it is excluded from the reported set by the rule
of Section III-B. Separately, an earlier analysis concluded that resolution
fails beyond about 0.4 mm of depth because the two imprints merge. At 2.50 mm
resolution continues to 0.9 mm in eight units. That conclusion holds only for
spacings of 1.10 to 2.00 mm.

[IV-B8]
The narrow-gap verdicts sit on the signal gate, and we report the sensitivity
rather than tuning it away. Lowering the gate from 2.5 to 2.0 grey levels takes
the number of units resolving 1.25 mm from five to nine. Half a grey level
corresponds to about 0.01 mm of indentation. The Rayleigh threshold of 0.265 is
an optical convention and the 2.5-level gate is justified in [IV-B2]; neither
was moved after seeing the data, but a reader should know that the limiting
spacing of a depth-referenced unit is a threshold-sensitive number in a way
that its resolved depth range is not.

[IV-B9]
Three limits bound this section. The marker-bearing arm was not measured for
spatial resolution at all, so the headline variable of this paper has no
three-principle comparison -- only the two. The photometric limit is a bound,
as [IV-B3] states, and closing it needs probes finer than 0.10 mm. And each
verdict rests on a single frame per depth, so there is no within-depth scatter;
consistency across depths is the only internal check available.


C. Maximum Measurable Force

[IV-C1]
What bounds force estimation is not where the gel fails but where the image
stops changing. Above that load a deeper press returns the same picture, so no
method that reads force from an image can separate two forces above it. We
measure that point on every unit rather than extrapolating it from a
calibration ladder; where both were available the extrapolation disagreed with
the measurement by up to a factor of two.

[IV-C2]
A depth-controlled ramp presses the 8 mm sphere into the gel in steps,
recording force and image at each one. Image change is reduced to a single
number -- the mean absolute difference from the undeformed reference over the
central half of the frame -- and the response is the slope of that number per
newton (levels/N, least squares over a three-step window). Saturation is
declared when the response falls below a threshold on two steps, and the
ceiling is the force at the first of them. No criterion, including the depth
backstop at 0.9 times gel thickness, is allowed to end a ramp below 10 N, so
every unit is pushed against the same floor and the ceilings are comparable.

[IV-C3]
Two thresholds are reported and the difference between them matters. The
relative criterion -- 15 % of the unit's own peak response -- is the rule the
ramp itself ran, and it states the operating limit of a single unit correctly.
It cannot compare units: a unit whose response is weak has a correspondingly
low threshold and therefore reports a high ceiling. The replicate data of
Section III-B makes this concrete rather than hypothetical. In the two pairs
whose peak responses differ most, by 35 % and 39 %, the ceilings differ by 21 %
and 20 %, and in both it is the weaker-responding unit that reads higher; the
two units with a confirmed light leak, which are weak for a known reason, read
24 % and 25 % above their twins. Comparison across principles therefore uses an
absolute criterion, a fixed levels/N threshold, recomputed from the same
recorded ramps.

[IV-C4]
Table V gives both. Absolute thresholds at which more than 10 % of ramps end
before crossing are excluded, because discarding truncated units biases the
median of the survivors downward and the truncated units are systematically the
high-ceiling ones; that rules out 0.2 and 0.5 levels/N, where up to 88 % of the
depth-referenced ramps are truncated. The two admissible thresholds order the
principles the same way and both separate them significantly, so the choice of
threshold is not producing the result.

  Truncation at the two absolute thresholds is 6 % and 0 %.

[IV-C5]
The depth-referenced principle reads further, and the relative criterion
understates by how much: 1.8-fold under the rule the ramp ran, 4.3- to 6.6-fold
under the absolute criteria. In the reported set the separation is complete --
every depth-referenced unit exceeds every photometric one, 27.5-63.2 N against
11.8-22.4 N -- but this is a property of the reported set and not of the
principles. With both replicates of every cell the ranges overlap, 24.6-63.2
against 11.1-23.4 N. An individual unit therefore still cannot be assigned to a
principle from its ceiling alone; it is the medians that separate.

[IV-C6]
Thickness acts on the two principles in opposite directions (Table VI). Over the
depth-referenced units the ceiling rises with thickness (Spearman rho +0.63,
p 0.068) and most steeply in the hard series, 27.5 to 43.3 to 63.2 N; over the
photometric units it falls (rho -0.79, p 0.011), and it falls in the
marker-bearing arm as well (rho -0.53, p 0.145). The gap between the principles
therefore widens with thickness.

[IV-C7]
The saturation depths explain the reversal, and they behave far more simply
than the forces. Depth follows thickness in all three arms (rho +0.90 to +0.95,
p < 0.001) and follows hardness in none of them (rho -0.05 to +0.11, p > 0.5).
Where the saturation depth falls relative to the layer is what separates the
principles. The depth-referenced units saturate at 2.0 to 4.7 times their own
gel thickness, so the indenter has long since compressed the gel against the
rigid backing and what resists it is the substrate as much as the elastomer;
the mechanical properties of the gel enter the ceiling directly, and a thicker
gel resists further. The photometric units saturate at 0.60 to 1.32 times
thickness, well before the gel approaches any mechanical limit -- the optics
saturate first, and a thicker layer images the contact less sharply, so it stops
answering sooner. Saturation depth is set by how thick the layer is; hardness
only changes the force needed to reach it.

[IV-C8]
Hardness and thickness multiply rather than add. Within the depth-referenced
series the marginal correlation between ceiling and hardness is not significant
at one unit per cell (rho +0.37, p 0.329), which is the wrong question to ask
of this data: the ratio of the hard ceiling to the soft ceiling is 0.99 at
1 mm, 1.43 at 2 mm and 2.04 at 3 mm. At 1 mm, forty durometer points of
hardness buy nothing at all. The mechanism is the one above -- at 1 mm the
ceiling depth is four to five times the gel thickness, so the load is carried
by a backing that is identical in every unit and does not know the formulation,
and the gel's own stiffness emerges only as the layer thickens. The design
consequence is narrow but concrete: in a depth-referenced sensor a harder gel
raises the maximum measurable force only if the layer is thick enough for the
gel to carry the load.

[IV-C9]
Hardness cannot be compared across the two principles, and that is a property
of our specimens rather than of the sensors. The depth-referenced series spans
40 durometer points and the photometric series six (Section III-B). Applying
the depth-referenced sensitivity at 3 mm, 0.77 N per durometer point, to the
photometric six-point range predicts a spread of 4.6 N against a measured 3.3
N; at 2 mm it predicts 2.4 N against a measured 3.8 N. The measured variation
is the size the wider series would lead one to expect, and at 2 mm it is
larger. The photometric series is therefore not insensitive to hardness -- we
barely varied its hardness. Whether the two principles differ in hardness
sensitivity is a question this design cannot answer.

[IV-C10]
A high ceiling and a strong response are two readings of one property, not two
independent merits. Among the depth-referenced 3 mm units the peak response
orders soft above medium above hard, 6.44, 5.63 and 3.23 levels/N, exactly
opposite to their ceilings of 31.0, 38.5 and 63.2 N. A softer gel deforms more
under the same load, so its image changes more per newton and it reaches the
saturation threshold sooner. A fabrication guideline has to say which of the
two is being bought.

[IV-C11]
Four limits belong with these numbers. First, the marker-bearing arm was ramped
only with the 4 mm sphere and cannot enter Tables V and VI. On its own ruler
its ceilings run from 6.2 to 20.5 N with a median of 9.2 N and fall with
thickness as the markerless photometric units do; that is as far as the
comparison can be taken. Second, the two spheres do not convert. Across 18
units measured with both, the force ratio scatters from 1.50 to 3.26 with a
median of 2.37, where Hertzian contact predicts the square root of the radius
ratio, 1.41, and two replicates of one specification give 1.98 and 3.26. The
depth ratio, by contrast, has a median of 0.99 and is indistinguishable from
unity (p 0.858): the saturation depth is a property of the unit, and the force
at that depth is set by the probe. The same 18 pairs rule out a field-of-view
confound -- the 8 mm sphere leaves an imprint 1.2 to 1.4 times larger at equal
depth, yet it saturated shallower in only 10 of 18 units (binomial p 0.815).
Third, the absolute criterion has not been recomputed on the 27-unit set; the
two absolute rows of Table V include the second replicate of every cell.
Fourth, one depth-referenced gel was destroyed by an early ramp, so its cell is
represented by the surviving replicate rather than by r1.
